How to Detect a Deepfake Video Call: 7 Checks That Actually Work (2026)

Updated October 19, 2026 · 8 min read

In September 2026, the FBI issued a public service announcement with a staggering figure: between January 2025 and July 2026, nearly 61,000 Americans filed complaints about callers who sounded and looked exactly like federal agents, judges, or diplomats. None of them were real. Combined losses topped $1.6 billion.

A month later, advertising giant WPP confirmed its CEO had been targeted by a deepfake scam — fraudsters cloned his voice, set up a fake Microsoft Teams meeting, and tried to solicit money from an executive. The attempt failed only because someone stayed vigilant.

These are not isolated incidents. Researchers project deepfake identity fraud will surge 495% in 2026, and one industry report estimates deepfake-enabled financial fraud has grown into a $40 billion annual market. Meanwhile, 62% of organizations say they were hit by a deepfake attack in the past 12 months.

The uncomfortable truth: you can no longer trust your eyes and ears on a video call. But you can learn to verify. Here are seven checks that actually work.

Check 1: Ask for a spontaneous physical action

This is the single most effective real-time test. Ask the person to do something unscripted: turn their head fully to one side, raise a hand in front of their face, or stand up briefly.

Why it works: real-time deepfakes are rendered frame by frame. Sharp head turns, occlusions (a hand passing in front of the face), and profile angles routinely break the illusion — ear geometry distorts, the jawline wobbles, or the face briefly "slides." A legitimate caller will just do it. A deepfake operator will stall, deflect, or the video will glitch.

Check 2: Watch the blink rate and micro-expressions

Humans blink every 4–6 seconds with natural irregularity. Many real-time deepfake models still struggle here — the person on screen may blink too rarely, too mechanically, or not at all. Also watch for emotional mismatch: a smile that doesn't reach the eyes, or facial movements slightly delayed relative to the voice. Don't rely on this check alone — models improve constantly, and a bad connection can produce similar artifacts.

Check 3: Inspect lighting, edges, and the background

Look at where the face meets the hair, neck, and background. Deepfakes often show faint blurring, warping, or flickering along these boundaries. Check whether the lighting on the face matches the room. AI-generated backgrounds can contain subtle impossibilities — text dissolving into gibberish, bending doorframes, shadows falling the wrong way.

Check 4: Listen for audio tells

Voice cloning needs as little as three seconds of sample audio. But real-time cloned audio still betrays itself:

  • Flat affect — AI voices often lack natural emotional variation, especially under urgency (exactly when scammers apply pressure).
  • Lip-sync drift — even a fraction-of-a-second mismatch between lip movement and audio is a strong signal.
  • Odd pacing — unnatural pauses, missing breaths, a cadence that feels "read" rather than spoken.

Check 5: Verify through a channel the call didn't touch

This is the check the FBI actually recommends — and the most important one on this list. Don't try to out-detect the AI. Out-maneuver it.

If your "CEO" is on a video call asking for an urgent wire transfer: say you need to step away, hang up, and call them back on a number you already had — not one provided during the call. Message them on internal chat. Walk to their office. A real executive will respect the caution; a scammer will manufacture urgency ("it has to happen NOW").

The textbook case: in 2024, a finance worker transferred about $25.6 million after a video call in which every participant — including the CFO — was a deepfake. A single callback to a known number would have stopped it.

Check 6: Examine metadata and content credentials

For received video files (not live calls), check metadata. The C2PA Content Credentials standard — backed by Adobe, Microsoft, and others — embeds tamper-evident provenance data, a "nutrition label" for media. Its absence doesn't prove a fake, but verifiable presence is strong evidence of authenticity. Note: the EU's AI transparency rules, in force since August 2026, require disclosure of AI-generated content.

Check 7: Run it through a free detection tool

Automated detectors can catch manipulations invisible to the human eye — but treat their verdicts as leads, not verdicts. Academic benchmarks have shown many research-grade detectors perform near chance on deepfakes actually circulating online, versus the lab datasets they were trained on.

Free tools worth knowing

  • F-Secure Deepfake Detector (free, launched Sept 2026) — upload an image or video for an AI-manipulation likelihood score. From a 35-year-old security brand.
  • DeepFake-O-Meter (free, academic) — University at Buffalo's forensics lab; runs your file through dozens of open-source detectors and shows each score, including disagreement between models.
  • Trend Micro ScamCheck (free app) — includes an AI video scan for screen recordings of suspicious calls.

No detector replaces Check 5. If a tool says "likely real" but your gut says otherwise, verify through another channel anyway.

What to do if you're on a deepfake call right now

  1. Don't act on the request. Money, credentials, a 2FA code — pause. The attack depends on the pause feeling rude.
  2. Say you need to switch to a verified channel. A real colleague accepts a callback. A scammer resists.
  3. Hang up and call back on a number from your own records — not one the caller gave you.
  4. Report it. In the US, file at ic3.gov even if no money moved.

The bottom line

Deepfake detection is an arms race, and the attackers currently have momentum. No single visual trick will keep you safe forever — models improve every quarter. What doesn't change is the defense that actually works: slow down and verify outside the channel. Faking a face is now cheap. Picking up the phone to call back is still free.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a video call be deepfaked in real time?

Yes. Real-time face-swapping runs on consumer hardware in 2026, and voice cloning needs only seconds of sample audio.

How accurate are free deepfake detectors?

Useful as a second opinion, not a verdict. Always pair automated checks with out-of-channel verification.

What should I do if my boss asks for money on a video call?

Pause, hang up, and call back on a number you already had — or message them on your company's internal system.

Are deepfakes illegal?

Fraud and impersonation for financial gain are criminalized in a growing number of countries; the EU's 2026 AI transparency rules require disclosure of AI-generated content.
